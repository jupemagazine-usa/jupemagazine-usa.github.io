A Tribute

The National Monument to Freedom — Montgomery, Alabama

In November 2026, Byron Jupiter stood before the National Monument to Freedom in EJI's Freedom Monument Sculpture Park, Montgomery, Alabama — a copper-toned wall carrying roughly 122,000 surnames chosen by formerly enslaved people and recorded in the 1870 census, the first census to list African Americans as free people. Among the names: JUPITER. This page is the tribute.

01

The wall

The massive copper-toned wall carries roughly 122,000 surnames — names chosen by formerly enslaved people and recorded in the 1870 census, the first United States census to list African Americans as free people. In the stills and the film of the visit, he stands in a flat cap and sunglasses before the monument, examining the engraved names and pointing at specific entries.

02

The name

The monument's interactive guide carries the surname roll. Scrolled to the J-U names, it highlights the surname JUPITER — the name he carries, chosen in freedom and recorded in 1870.

03

The flowers

The accompanying film shows a woman carefully placing flowers at the base of the curved black wall that bears the monument's dedication text. Placing flowers at the monument is an intentional part of the site's design: according to the Equal Justice Initiative, visitors can honor the people named on the wall by placing flowers in a stream that runs beside the monument.

04

What the flowers say

White flowers traditionally signify purity, peace, reverence, and remembrance — in memorial contexts, the restored dignity of the soul, free from suffering. Red flowers stand for deep love, honor, courage, and sacrifice, and can evoke the blood and cost of struggle. Together, red and white read as love and remembrance.

The offering echoes the monument's own inscription, which holds that those who were enslaved still found the capacity to love, to dream, to nurture new life, and to triumph. Beauty and care placed at the base of a wall of names: a quiet assertion that life and remembrance continue.

Flowers have long marked African American memorial practice. Early post-Civil War commemorations — sometimes cited as precursors to Memorial Day — saw Black communities decorating graves with flowers, and hardy periwinkle was used to mark the resting places of the formerly enslaved, an emblem of endurance that still helps locate those sites today.
